What to Ask a Franchisor About the Supply Chain Before You Sign

The franchise fee gets most of the attention during negotiations, yet the supply arrangement is what decides whether a branch earns money month after month.

Many first-time franchisees in the Philippines spend weeks comparing franchise fees and package inclusions, then sign an agreement without ever asking how the ingredients, packaging and stock will reach their store. That gap matters because a franchise outlet usually buys most of what it sells from the franchisor or from suppliers the franchisor approves, which means the supply contract quietly shapes every margin the owner will see.

Who sets the supply prices, and how often can they change?

The first question worth putting to any franchisor is who controls the price list for required supplies and under what conditions that list can move. Some systems publish a fixed schedule that changes only once or twice a year with written notice, while others reserve the right to adjust prices whenever their own costs rise, leaving the franchisee to absorb the difference without any room to raise menu prices on their own.

A careful buyer asks to see the current supply price list alongside the suggested retail prices, then works out the gross margin on the best selling items before anything is signed. It is also fair to ask whether the franchisor adds a markup on top of what it pays its own suppliers, because that markup can function as a second royalty that never appears in the headline royalty rate.

Can the franchisee buy anything locally?

Exclusive purchasing clauses protect brand consistency, which is reasonable for a signature sauce or a proprietary powder, but they become a burden when they cover ordinary items like cups, tissue, cooking oil or cleaning supplies that a nearby wholesaler sells for less. Prospective owners should ask exactly which items must come from the franchisor, which may come from approved third parties, and whether there is a process for getting a local supplier approved when it meets the same quality standard.

What are the minimum orders and how do they fit a small branch?

Minimum order quantities are easy to overlook when a business plan assumes steady sales, yet they can tie up a surprising amount of cash in a slow month or during the first weeks after opening. A food cart in a provincial town may sell a fraction of what a mall kiosk in a busy city sells, so a minimum order designed for high volume outlets can leave a small operator holding stock that approaches its expiry date.

The useful follow up questions are whether the minimum is measured per delivery or per month, whether it scales with branch size, and what happens to perishable items that expire before they are sold. Buyers working within a modest capital, such as those browsing franchise options around 200,000 pesos, often find that stock commitments matter as much as the initial package price.

How does delivery to the provinces actually work?

A franchisor that runs smoothly in Metro Manila may struggle to serve branches in Mindanao or the Visayas, and the cost of that distance usually lands on the franchisee. It is worth asking where the nearest commissary or warehouse sits, how often deliveries reach a given province, who pays freight and handling, and how long an order takes from placement to arrival when typhoons or port delays interrupt shipping.

Owners considering smaller cities should press for specifics, since a schedule of one delivery every two weeks forces a branch to carry more inventory and more storage space than a weekly route would. Anyone exploring lower budget franchises in Koronadal or similar provincial markets benefits from asking existing franchisees in the region how reliable their deliveries have been.

What happens when supply runs short?

Stockouts are where supply chain weaknesses become visible to customers, so a buyer should ask how the franchisor allocates scarce items among branches and whether outlets are allowed to source a substitute temporarily. Retail formats feel this sharply, and those comparing convenience store franchises should ask how many product lines are centrally supplied, how quickly empty shelves get restocked and whether the system tracks fill rates for each branch.

Putting the answers side by side

A well run supply chain is one of the real advantages of joining an established system, so these questions are not meant to scare anyone away from franchising. The goal is to understand the full cost of operating a branch, not just the cost of opening one, and to spot the arrangements that might squeeze margins once the excitement of the launch fades.
